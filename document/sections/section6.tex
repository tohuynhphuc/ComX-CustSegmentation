\section{Context \& Dashboard Objectives}
\label{sec:bi_context}

\subsection{Business Context}
\label{subsec:bi_business_context}
After completing the data warehouse and running the K-Means clustering model, our team needed an interactive tool to show the results to decision-makers. In the database, customer data and cluster numbers are stored in tables, which are difficult for managers to read and understand quickly. Therefore, we built a Business Intelligence (BI) dashboard using Microsoft Power BI.

The dashboard uses the current warehouse, but its \emph{live analytical view for reporting} is the
\texttt{FactCustomerSnapshot} slice at \texttt{SnapshotDateKey = 20140630}, rather than the physical
\texttt{FactCustomerRFMLive} table. This matters because the latter deliberately contains purchase-time recency of zero
for refreshed customers, while a common month-end date is needed to compare inactivity. At this stage, the dashboard is
integrated but Page 1 remains subject to final team refinement. The three pages focus on:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Executive Overview:} Help company directors monitor total sales, customer numbers, yearly trends, and early warnings about customer churn.
  \item \textbf{Customer Demographics:} Help marketing and sales teams understand customer profiles, such as location, income level, age group, gender, and job types.
  \item \textbf{RFM Segmentation and Strategy:} Help the CRM team see how each customer cluster behaves in terms of Recency, Frequency, and Monetary value, and choose the right marketing strategy for each group.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Visualization Principles}
\label{subsec:bi_principles}
To ensure that the dashboards are clear, consistent, and easy to interpret, we applied data visualization principles
discussed in the course and in \textcite{wilke2019fundamentals}. The selection of chart types was based on the
characteristics of the data and the analytical purpose of each visualization.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Treemaps:} Used to represent categorical distributions, such as customer occupations, without overcrowding the visualization.
  \item \textbf{100\% Stacked Column Charts:} Used to compare the relative proportions of customer segments across different time periods, highlighting changes in segment composition.
  \item \textbf{Age Distribution Charts:} Customers are grouped into age ranges to provide a clearer overview of the demographic distribution.
  \item \textbf{Bubble Charts:} Used to visualize the relationship between Recency and Monetary values, with bubble size representing the number of customers in each segment.
  \item \textbf{Consistent Segment Colors:}
        Each customer segment is assigned a fixed color across all three dashboard pages, allowing users to identify and compare segments more easily. The colors are defined as follows:
        \begin{itemize}
          \item \textcolor[HTML]{A82020}{\textbf{Champions:}}
                Red (\#A82020)
          \item \textcolor[HTML]{22593D}{\textbf{High-Value / Cooling Off:}}
                Dark Green (\#22593D)
          \item \textcolor[HTML]{D97F1A}{\textbf{Recent / Promising:}}
                Orange (\#D97F1A)
          \item \textcolor[HTML]{259696}{\textbf{Low-Spend / Occasional:}}
                Teal (\#259696)
          \item \textcolor[HTML]{4C2773}{\textbf{Lost / Dormant:}}
                Purple (\#4C2773)
        \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
\subsection{Dashboard Structure}
\label{subsec:bi_hierarchy}
The Power BI report consists of three pages, each serving a different analytical purpose:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Page 1 -- Executive Customer \& Segmentation Overview:} Presents key performance indicators and trends in customer activity and segment distribution.
  \item \textbf{Page 2 -- Customer Demographics \& Persona Profiling:} Examines demographic characteristics and allows users to explore the profiles of individual customer segments.
  \item \textbf{Page 3 -- Customer RFM Segmentation \& Behavior:} Compares the purchasing behavior of customer segments through RFM metrics and visualizations.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Core Executive KPIs}
\label{subsec:bi_page1_kpis}
At the top of Page 1, we place four key metrics:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Total Customer Spend (\$15.62M):} Sum of the 365-day customer monetary values in the dashboard reference snapshot (\$15,615,354.05), rather than verified lifetime revenue.
  \item \textbf{Total Customers (18,485):} Distinct customers in the imported 30 June 2014 reporting view; this is not a claim about all possible customers in the source database.
  \item \textbf{Inactive Customers ($> 100$ Days) (12,895):} Customers whose snapshot recency exceeds 100 days as of 30 June 2014. This makes up \textbf{69.76\%} of all customers. In the bicycle retail industry, bikes are durable goods, so people do not purchase every month. However, after 100 days (more than 3 months), if a buyer does not come back for small accessories or maintenance, they are at risk of leaving. Therefore, this KPI serves as an early-warning signal.
  \item \textbf{Repeat Customer Rate (14.96\%):} Calculated as the percentage of customers who have ordered at least two times:
        \begin{equation}
          \mathrm{Repeat\ Customer\ Rate} = \frac{\sum [ \mathrm{Customers\ with\ Frequency} \ge 2 ]}{\mathrm{Total\ Customers}} = \frac{2{,}765}{18{,}485} = 14.96\%
        \end{equation}
        This indicates that 85.04\% do not have at least two orders \emph{within the trailing 365-day measurement window}. It does not establish that these customers purchased only once over their entire history.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Visual Charts and Key Findings}
\label{subsec:bi_page1_insights}
\subsubsection{1. Customer Segment Mix by Year (2011--2014)}
We selected a 100\% Stacked Column Chart to show the proportion of each customer segment over the four operational
years:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{2011 Baseline:} In the first year, CompanyX focused on establishing its retail presence. \textit{Recent / Promising} customers constituted the largest group at \textbf{39.14\%}, followed by \textit{Champions} at \textbf{26.65\%} and \textit{High-Value / Cooling Off} at \textbf{24.49\%}. The \textit{Low-Spend / Occasional} and \textit{Lost / Dormant} segments formed only a small portion (roughly 9.7\% combined).
  \item \textbf{2012 Transition:} As earlier buyers matured, the proportion of \textit{High-Value / Cooling Off} customers increased to \textbf{31.57\%}. However, buyers who did not place repeat orders transitioned into \textit{Lost / Dormant}, which rose to \textbf{20.13\%}. The share of \textit{Champions} stood at \textbf{15.25\%}, while \textit{Recent / Promising} made up \textbf{27.02\%}.
  \item \textbf{2013 Product Diversification:} In 2013, the business began offering bike accessories and apparel. As a result, the \textit{Low-Spend / Occasional} segment grew substantially to \textbf{12.48\%}. The \textit{High-Value / Cooling Off} group remained steady at \textbf{31.39\%}, and \textit{Champions} accounted for \textbf{20.81\%}.
  \item \textbf{2014 Mass Market Expansion:} By 2014, the proportion of \textit{Low-Spend / Occasional} shoppers more than doubled to \textbf{26.78\%}. Meanwhile, \textit{High-Value / Cooling Off} represented \textbf{27.44\%}, \textit{Recent / Promising} was \textbf{27.34\%}, and \textit{Champions} made up \textbf{11.05\%}. The \textit{Lost / Dormant} group dropped to \textbf{7.39\%}.
\end{itemize}
These figures illustrate an important strategic shift: while CompanyX was originally a niche retailer of premium bicycles dominated by high-value buyers in 2011--2012, it successfully expanded to general everyday shoppers in 2013--2014. Nevertheless, this expansion brought a large volume of one-time or low-spending buyers.

\subsubsection{2. Rolling Customer Spend at Monthly Snapshots (2011--2014)}
The Area Chart follows the sum of trailing-365-day customer monetary values at successive monthly snapshot dates (July
2011 to June 2014). It shows a \emph{rolling annual spending measure sampled monthly}, not independent sales revenue
earned within each calendar month:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Early Stage (2011--Mid 2012):} The rolling spend measure started at a moderate level, which was typical for a new business with a limited product catalog.
  \item \textbf{Rapid Acceleration (Mid 2012--End 2013):} Starting in the second half of 2012, rolling customer monetary totals increased significantly. The chart shows strong upward momentum throughout 2013, reaching a high rolling monetary value towards the end of 2013.
  \item \textbf{Seasonality Trends:} Changes in the rolling total may reflect both newly included purchases and older purchases leaving the 365-day window. A claim about monthly seasonality would require a separate measure of sales booked within each individual month.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{3. Customer Spend \& Volume by Segment}
This Dual-Axis Combo Chart displays total sales as bars on the left axis and customer counts as points on the right
axis:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Spending Concentration (rather than a strict 80/20 split):}
        \begin{itemize}
          \item The \textit{High-Value / Cooling Off} cluster generates \textbf{\$11.2M} (71.53\% of total revenue) from 7,357
                customers (39.80\% of the customer base).
          \item The \textit{Champions} cluster contributes \textbf{\$4.0M} (25.81\% of total revenue) from 2,377 customers (12.86\%).
          \item Together, these two premium segments contribute \textbf{97.34\%} of the snapshot's rolling-365-day monetary total
                (\$15.2M out of \$15.62M), while making up only \textbf{52.66\%} of all customers (9,734 people).
        \end{itemize}
  \item \textbf{The Low-Monetary Contrast:}
        In comparison, the \textit{Low-Spend / Occasional} group contains 7,175 customers (38.82\% of the total base) but brings in only \textbf{\$0.35M} (2.25\% of revenue). Similarly, \textit{Recent / Promising} accounts for \textbf{\$0.07M} (0.42\%), and \textit{Lost / Dormant} accounts for \textbf{\$0.00}.
  \item \textbf{Key Takeaway:}
        These figures motivate prioritizing high-value retention campaigns; whether a campaign is more profitable than acquisition must be assessed separately using cost and response data.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Dashboard Interactivity and Slicer Architecture}
\label{subsec:bi_page1_slicer_design}
In our Power BI solution, an interactive dropdown slicer (\textit{Filter by Segment}) is placed in the top-right corner of each dashboard page. However, during the dashboard development, an important architectural decision was required for Page 1:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{The Macro Trend Conflict:} The two longitudinal visuals---\textit{Customer Segment Mix by Year} and \textit{Rolling Customer Spend Trend}---rely on historical snapshot data from the periodic snapshot table (\texttt{FactCustomerSnapshot}) to show the whole company's evolution. In particular, the 100\% stacked column chart displays the structural proportions among all five segments. If a single segment is filtered, the multi-segment composition collapses to 100\%, eliminating its analytical purpose.
  \item \textbf{Implementation of Edit Interactions:} Following standard Business Intelligence best practices, we accessed the \textit{Format $\rightarrow$ Edit Interactions} menu in Power BI and set the interaction from the segment slicer to \textbf{None} for these two macro trend visuals. As a result, users can filter the top KPI cards and the segment combo chart without breaking the overall company baseline timeline.
\end{itemize}

        \begin{itemize}
          \item \textit{Occupation Disparity:} \textit{Skilled Manual} workers heavily dominate at \textbf{37.79\%} (339 buyers), while \textit{Management} drops to its lowest proportion at \textbf{8.70\%}.

        \end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}
