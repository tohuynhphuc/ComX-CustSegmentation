\section{Context \& Dashboard Objectives}
\label{sec:bi_context}

\subsection{Business Context}
\label{subsec:bi_business_context}
Following the construction of the dimensional data warehouse and the K-Means clustering model, our team built an interactive Business Intelligence dashboard using Microsoft Power BI. While underlying SQL tables store comprehensive transaction records and cluster assignments, business managers require intuitive visual summaries to track performance and guide operational decisions.

The dashboard connects directly to the data warehouse, utilizing the monthly snapshot slice at \texttt{SnapshotDateKey = 20140630} as its primary analytical reporting dataset. This fixed cutoff date provides a standardized baseline to compare recency, spending, and inactivity across the entire customer base. The Power BI report is organized into three interconnected pages:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Executive Overview:} Enables senior directors to monitor company-wide customer volume, total revenue, longitudinal segment migration, and customer inactivity alerts.
    \item \textbf{Customer Demographics:} Assists marketing and sales teams in profiling customers by geographic location, income bracket, age distribution, gender, and occupation.
    \item \textbf{RFM Segmentation and Behavior:} Provides CRM managers with detailed behavioral metrics (Recency, Frequency, Monetary) across the five clusters to design targeted retention strategies.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Visualization Principles}
\label{subsec:bi_principles}
To ensure clarity, visual hierarchy, and intuitive navigation, the dashboard adheres to established data visualization standards \parencite{wilke2019fundamentals}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Treemaps for Categorical Hierarchies:} Used for multi-category distributions such as customer occupations, offering superior readability compared to crowded pie charts.
    \item \textbf{100\% Stacked Column Charts for Proportions Over Time:} Applied to illustrate the structural evolution of customer segments across the four operational years (2011--2014).
    \item \textbf{Binned Histograms for Demographic Spans:} Customer ages are grouped into distinct demographic intervals ($<30$, $30-40$, $41-50$, $51-60$, $60+$) to reveal primary customer life stages.
    \item \textbf{Bubble Charts for Multi-Dimensional Relationships:} Plotted with Recency on the X-axis, Monetary spend on the Y-axis, customer volume as bubble size, and benchmark reference lines to segment buyers into four strategic quadrants.
    \item \textbf{Consistent Visual Palette:} Segment colors remain strictly synchronized across all three pages to prevent cognitive friction:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textcolor[rgb]{0.70, 0.13, 0.13}{\textbf{1. Champions:}} Crimson Red (\#A82020)
        \item \textcolor[rgb]{0.13, 0.35, 0.24}{\textbf{2. High-Value / Cooling Off:}} Forest Green (\#22593D)
        \item \textcolor[rgb]{0.85, 0.50, 0.10}{\textbf{3. Recent / Promising:}} Vibrant Orange (\#D97F1A)
        \item \textcolor[rgb]{0.15, 0.60, 0.60}{\textbf{4. Low-Spend / Occasional:}} Deep Teal (\#259696)
        \item \textcolor[rgb]{0.30, 0.15, 0.45}{\textbf{5. Lost / Dormant:}} Royal Purple (\#4C2773)
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Core Executive KPIs}
\label{subsec:bi_page1_kpis}
The top ribbon highlights four primary performance indicators:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Total Customer Spend (\$15.46M):} The total customer monetary value recorded in the warehouse over the trailing 365-day observation window (\$15,455,666.27).
    \item \textbf{Total Customers (18,485):} The distinct count of individual retail customers captured in the reporting view as of 30 June 2014.
    \item \textbf{Inactive Customers ($> 100$ Days) (12,825):} Customers whose recency exceeds 100 days without a purchase, representing \textbf{69.38\%} of the customer base. Because bicycles and high-end gear are durable products, shoppers do not purchase monthly; however, exceeding 100 days without buying consumables, accessories, or service signals immediate risk of churn.
    \item \textbf{Repeat Customer Rate (14.74\%):} The percentage of customers with two or more orders in the trailing 365 days:
    \begin{equation}
        \mathrm{Repeat\ Customer\ Rate} = \frac{\sum [ \mathrm{Customers\ with\ Frequency} \ge 2 ]}{\mathrm{Total\ Customers}} = \frac{2{,}724}{18{,}485} = 14.74\%
    \end{equation}
    This metric demonstrates that 85.26\% of active buyers placed only a single order during the year, highlighting strong commercial potential for re-engagement.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Visual Charts and Key Findings}
\label{subsec:bi_page1_insights}
\subsubsection{1. Customer Segment Mix by Year (2011--2014)}
The 100\% Stacked Column Chart illustrates the annual composition of customer segments over the four operating years:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{2011 Baseline:} In its initial operating year, CompanyX focused on premium bicycles. \textit{Recent / Promising} buyers made up the largest group at \textbf{37.89\%}, followed by \textit{Champions} at \textbf{29.47\%} and \textit{High-Value / Cooling Off} at \textbf{23.60\%}. The \textit{Low-Spend / Occasional} and \textit{Lost / Dormant} segments accounted for the remaining 9.04\% combined.
    \item \textbf{2012 Maturation:} As initial buyers aged without repeat purchases, the share of \textit{High-Value / Cooling Off} rose to \textbf{30.97\%}, while \textit{Recent / Promising} stood at \textbf{26.66\%}. \textit{Lost / Dormant} expanded to \textbf{19.96\%}, and \textit{Champions} represented \textbf{17.09\%}.
    \item \textbf{2013 Product Diversification:} CompanyX expanded its accessory and apparel offerings in 2013. The share of \textit{High-Value / Cooling Off} stabilized at \textbf{30.89\%}, \textit{Recent / Promising} was \textbf{23.59\%}, and \textit{Champions} accounted for \textbf{22.17\%}. Concurrently, \textit{Low-Spend / Occasional} buyers surged to \textbf{12.25\%}, while \textit{Lost / Dormant} was \textbf{11.10\%}.
    \item \textbf{2014 Mass Market Expansion:} By 2014, the proportion of \textit{Low-Spend / Occasional} shoppers reached \textbf{26.79\%}. Concurrently, \textit{High-Value / Cooling Off} accounted for \textbf{27.46\%}, \textit{Recent / Promising} stood at \textbf{27.30\%}, \textit{Champions} represented \textbf{10.98\%}, and \textit{Lost / Dormant} was \textbf{7.48\%}.
\end{itemize}
These shifts demonstrate CompanyX's transition from a high-end bicycle specialist in 2011 into a diversified mass-market sports retailer by 2014.

\subsubsection{2. Monthly Customer Spend Trend (2011--2014)}
The Area Chart plots trailing 365-day customer spend sampled at monthly snapshot intervals:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Steady Build-Up (2011--Mid 2012):} Reflects steady early-stage growth as the initial customer foundation was established.
    \item \textbf{Rapid Acceleration (Mid 2012--Late 2013):} Rolling customer spend climbed rapidly throughout 2013, driven by catalog expansion and increasing order volumes.
    \item \textbf{Stabilization (Late 2013--2014):} Spending stabilized at a mature plateau near \$15.5M annually.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{3. Customer Spend \& Volume by Segment}
The Dual-Axis Combo Chart highlights spending concentration across clusters:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Extreme Revenue Concentration:}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item The \textit{High-Value / Cooling Off} segment accounts for \textbf{\$11.10M} (71.83\% of total spend) across 7,305 customers (39.52\% of all buyers).
        \item The \textit{Champions} cluster contributes \textbf{\$3.94M} (25.50\% of total spend) from 2,339 customers (12.65\%).
        \item Combined, these two premium segments generate \textbf{97.33\%} of company revenue while comprising \textbf{52.17\%} of the customer base.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{The Low-Spend Contrast:}
    In contrast, \textit{Low-Spend / Occasional} buyers make up 38.52\% of customers (7,121 individuals) but generate only \textbf{\$0.35M} (2.25\% of revenue), while \textit{Recent / Promising} accounts for \textbf{\$0.06M} (0.41\%) and \textit{Lost / Dormant} contributes \textbf{\$0.00}.
    \item \textbf{Strategic Takeaway:} Marketing investments must prioritize retaining high-value buyers, as losing a small fraction of cooling-off customers would severely impact total revenue.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Key Findings from Segment Drill-Down}
Filtering each segment on Page 2 highlights clear differences in purchasing power, age distribution, and professional background across the five clusters:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Champions (Affluent White-Collar Repeat Buyers):}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textit{High Earning Power:} Champions have the highest income level among all clusters. Buyers earning more than \$100,000 account for \textbf{14.02\%} (328 individuals), while \textbf{30.74\%} (719 individuals) earn between \$50,000 and \$75,000.
        \item \textit{White-Collar Dominance:} \textit{Professional} (38.86\%, 909 people) and \textit{Management} (18.98\%, 444 people) make up \textbf{57.85\%} of this group, showing that corporate earners drive repeat high-value purchases.
        \item \textit{Distinct Repeat Ordering:} This is the only segment where Average Spend per Customer (\$1,685.11) is substantially higher than Average Order Value (\textbf{\$661.88}), reflecting sustained repeat orders ($F \approx 2.55$).
        \item \textit{Age Concentration:} Heavily concentrated in the \textbf{41--50} age bracket (\textbf{38.26\%}, 895 buyers), followed by 51--60 (\textbf{27.92\%}, 653 buyers). These are established professionals investing in premium cycling and health.
    \end{itemize}

    \item \textbf{High-Value / Cooling Off (Single-Purchase Premium Buyers):}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textit{High Initial Basket Size:} Average Spend per Customer exactly equals Average Order Value at \textbf{\$1,519.72}, indicating that each customer made a single major bicycle purchase ($F=1.00$) approximately 5.8 months ago ($R=174.18$ days) and has not returned.
        \item \textit{Middle-Class Core:} The primary income brackets are \$50,001--\$75,000 (32.11\%, 2,346 buyers) and \$25,001--\$50,000 (31.62\%, 2,310 buyers), representing \textbf{63.74\%} of the cluster.
        \item \textit{Age Demographic:} Dominated by mature adults aged 41--50 (39.36\%, 2.9K buyers) and 51--60 (28.55\%, 2.1K buyers).
    \end{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Recent / Promising (Senior Entrants \& Skilled Technicians):}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textit{Senior Age Demographic:} The single largest age bracket in \textit{Recent / Promising} is \textbf{60+ years old} (\textbf{29.46\%}, 200 customers), highlighting senior cyclists and retirees as an emerging acquisition segment.
        \item \textit{Occupational Profile:} \textit{Skilled Manual} workers lead at \textbf{28.72\%} (195 people), followed closely by \textit{Professional} at 27.54\%.
        \item \textit{Early Repeat Signals:} With an AOV of \$77.22 and an average spend of \$95.85, a subset of customers have already completed second accessory orders within two weeks of their first purchase ($R = 13.24$ days).
    \end{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Low-Spend / Occasional (Budget-Conscious Accessory Shoppers):}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textit{Lower Income Skew:} Contains the largest share of entry-level earners, with buyers earning below \$25,000 accounting for \textbf{20.57\%} (1,465 individuals).
        \item \textit{Occupational Spread:} \textit{Manual} workers represent 16.7\% (1.2K people), the highest proportion among all active clusters.
        \item \textit{Age Profile:} Shows a bi-modal distribution split between seniors aged \textbf{60+} (\textbf{29.3\%}) and young adults aged 30--40 (18.1\%).
        \item \textit{Low Basket Size:} An average order value of \textbf{\$47.35} indicates frequent purchases of low-cost consumables and basic replacement parts.
    \end{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Lost / Dormant (Disengaged Lower-Income Buyers):}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textit{Occupational Composition:} \textit{Skilled Manual} workers heavily dominate at \textbf{37.76\%} (350 buyers), while \textit{Management} drops to \textbf{9.17\%} (85 buyers).
        \item \textit{Income Distribution:} Over \textbf{88.67\%} of members (822 buyers) earn under \$75,000 annually, with \textbf{42.61\%} (395 buyers) in the \$25,001--\$50,000 bracket.
        \item \textit{Prolonged Inactivity:} With \$0.00 in spend and an average recency of 608.69 days, these customers have completely stopped purchasing.
    \end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Quadrant Analysis}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{High spend, below-average recency --- Champions:}
    These customers bought recently ($R=109.74$ days) and spend the most ($M=\$1,685.11$). They are our best VIP customers.
    \item \textbf{High spend, moderately recent --- High-Value / Cooling Off:}
    These customers spent a lot ($M=\$1,519.72$), but their Recency is 174.18 days, which is close to the 190-day average. They are starting to cool down and need immediate attention before they leave.
    \item \textbf{Low spend, very recent --- Recent / Promising:}
    These are new customers who bought recently ($R=13.24$ days). Their spend is still small (\$95.85), but they have great potential to grow into Champions.
    \item \textbf{Low spend, long recency --- Low-Spend and Lost:}
    These customers have not bought for a long time and spend very little. The Lost group has not returned for over 600 days ($R=608.69$).
\end{enumerate}
